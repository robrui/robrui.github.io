A One-Character Bug in scipy.signal.find_peaks_cwt

scipy#25074  ·  April 2025  ·  ~15 min read
TL;DR: A single-character indexing bug in SciPy's CWT-based peak detector caused the SNR (signal-to-noise ratio) to be read from the end of each wavelet ridge instead of its maximum. The result: real peaks with weaker SNR were silently discarded. The fix was a one-line change from colIndices[0] → colIndices[-1] + a max() call over the ridge.

1. Background: Continuous Wavelet Transform for Peak Detection

The Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) is a fundamental tool in time-frequency analysis. Unlike the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), which uses a fixed window length, the CWT uses a scaleable window — the wavelet — that stretches and compresses to capture both high and low frequencies.

For a signal $x(t)$ and a mother wavelet $\psi(t)$, the CWT at scale $s$ and position $\tau$ is:

$$ X_w(s, \tau) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|s|}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) \; \psi^*\!\left(\frac{t - \tau}{s}\right) dt $$

The result is a 2D transform matrix $X_w[s, \tau]$ where each row corresponds to a scale (roughly, a frequency band) and each column to a time position. The ridge of a peak is the set of positions across scales where the transform magnitude is locally maximal — it traces the dominant frequency content of that peak through scale space.

scipy.signal.find_peaks_cwt implements the algorithm described in Du et al. (1994). It works in three steps:

  1. Transform: Compute the CWT of the signal using a Ricker (Mexican hat) wavelet at each scale.
  2. Ridge detection: Find local maxima across the scale dimension — these ridges identify candidate peaks.
  3. SNR thresholding: For each ridge, compute a signal-to-noise ratio and discard ridges below a threshold.

Step 3 is where the bug lived.

2. The Bug: SNR at the Wrong Position

Let's look at the actual source. The ridge-finding logic returns, for each detected ridge, a list of column indices — one per scale — that trace the ridge from the smallest scale to the largest: 

# Simplified from scipy/signal/_peak_finding.py
colIndices = ridge_lines  # list of column indices along the ridge
# colIndices[0] = position at smallest scale
# colIndices[-1] = position at largest scale

The original code computed the SNR as the transform value at the first element of this ridge: 

snr = abs(cwt[ridge[-1], colIndices[0]])  # ❌ BUG: position at smallest scale

Wait — ridge[-1] is the largest scale (last row index), but colIndices[0] is the smallest-scale position. The SNR ends up being the CWT coefficient at the wrong (scale, position) pair! The correct approach is to find the maximum along the ridge: 

snr = max(abs(cwt[ridge[-1], colIndices[-1]]),    # ✅ FIX: position at largest scale
           max(abs(cwt[r, c]) for r, c in zip(ridge, colIndices)))

Or, equivalently and more elegantly:

# Take the maximum CWT coefficient along the entire ridge
ridge_values = [abs(cwt[r, c]) for r, c in zip(ridge, colIndices)]
snr = max(ridge_values)

The impact of this bug is critical: for a peak whose energy is concentrated at larger scales (which is typical for broader peaks), the SNR at the smallest scale can be orders of magnitude smaller than the true ridge maximum. Peaks get silently dropped below the noise threshold — false negatives that corrupt downstream analysis.

The Actual Fix

Here's the precise diff from the merged PR: 

 # inside the ridge SNR computation loop
- snr = abs(cwt[ridge[-1], colIndices[0]])
+ # Take maximum along the ridge instead of just the endpoint
+ ridge_vals = [abs(cwt[r, c]) for r, c in zip(ridge, colIndices)]
+ snr = max(ridge_vals)

3. Visualizing the Problem

Let's reproduce the failure. Consider a signal with two Gaussian peaks at different amplitudes: 

import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import find_peaks_cwt

x = np.linspace(0, 10, 500)
signal = (np.exp(-(x - 3)**2)
          + 0.5 * np.exp(-(x - 7)**2)
          + 0.1 * np.random.randn(500))

peaks = find_peaks_cwt(signal, np.arange(1, 10))
print(f"Detected {len(peaks)} peaks at: {x[peaks].round(2)}")

With the buggy code, the SNR for the smaller peak (at $x \approx 7$) is calculated from the CWT coefficient at the ridge's starting position — where the wavelet convolution is weak — instead of its maximum. This often pushes the SNR below the acceptance threshold, and the peak vanishes from the result.

After the fix, the SNR accurately reflects the maximum energy along the ridge, and both peaks are correctly detected — provided they exceed the user's noise floor.

4. Why This Matters: A Closer Look at CWT Ridge SNR

The concept of a ridge in wavelet space is central to many CWT applications beyond peak finding:

  • Spectral estimation: Ridges trace instantaneous frequency (see Mallat's "ridge extraction" for reassignment methods).
  • Modulation analysis: In speech processing, formant trajectories appear as ridges in the CWT magnitude scalogram.
  • Skeleton denoising: Only coefficients on ridges are kept for noise reduction, relying on accurate SNR to separate signal from noise.

The ridge SNR is defined as the maximum of the CWT magnitude along the ridge:

$$ \text{SNR}_{\text{ridge}} = \max_{s \in \mathcal{R}} |X_w(s, \tau(s))| $$

where $\mathcal{R}$ is the set of scales belonging to the ridge and $\tau(s)$ is the position at scale $s$. Using any single point on the ridge — especially an endpoint — gives a potentially arbitrary underestimation.

For a Ricker wavelet, the transform of a Gaussian peak reaches its maximum at a scale proportional to the peak's width. A narrow peak peaks at small scales, a wide peak at large scales. If the ridge SNR is only evaluated at one endpoint, one of these peak types will be systematically penalized.

5. Impact & Who This Affects

scipy.signal.find_peaks_cwt is used across scientific domains:

  • Spectroscopy: Identifying emission/absorption lines in noisy spectra
  • Bio-signals: R-peak detection in ECGs, spike sorting in neural recordings
  • Seismology: Arrival time picking in seismic traces
  • Analytical chemistry: Chromatogram peak detection

Any user running find_peaks_cwt with a noise threshold was at risk of missing real peaks — especially broad ones whose ridge energy concentrated at larger scales. The fix restores the mathematically correct behavior: SNR is the maximum energy along the ridge, not an endpoint sample.

7. Why This Matters for Signal Processing Engineers

This bug is a cautionary tale about ridge-based analysis, a technique that's foundational to wavelet signal processing. The mistake — indexing into the ridge at the wrong position — is the kind of subtle off-by-one error that can silently corrupt results without crashing or warning.

For signal processing engineers applying to roles at Hugging Face, DeepMind, or anywhere that builds on scientific Python, understanding these internals isn't just trivia. It demonstrates:
